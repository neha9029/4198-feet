Great Salt Lake is not a fixed shape. It breathes with every foot of water. Today, at four thousand one hundred eighty-nine point six feet, so much of that water is gone.

In daylight, the loss almost disappears. What used to be lake looks like ordinary land. But the surface is holding a secret.

As the light warms toward tungsten, the hidden shoreline returns. Rose spreads across the north arm. Blue-green gathers across the south, through Bear River Bay and Farmington Bay.

This is the lake at four thousand one hundred ninety-eight feet. Not an imagined boundary, but a reconstruction from decades of Landsat water observations, calibrated to the lake's official relationship between elevation and area.

Managed evaporation ponds are left outside. The surrounding mountains rise from United States Geological Survey three D elevation data, smoothed and exaggerated four times.

Then daylight returns, and the vanished lake disappears again. The land has not moved. Only the light has changed. But now we have seen the space the lake should occupy, and how much is at stake.
